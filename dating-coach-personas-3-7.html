Sarah,

What you’re describing isn’t a bad luck streak — it’s a pattern of effort the apps make easy. The good news: every part of it has a fix. Here are the three answers most relevant to where you are right now.

1. On where to meet outside the apps

Two things, both at the same time. First: ask the people whose judgment you trust to introduce you. Not strangers — friends, colleagues, the few people who really know you. Tell them clearly what you’re looking for. Most adults don’t ask — and that’s why nothing happens.

Second: be out, and stay open. Take the class, go to the dinner, accept the invitation. Most of the meeting that actually leads somewhere happens in places and at times you don’t expect — but only if you’re there to receive it. Closed off at home, nothing finds you.

2. On the “text for weeks then disappear” pattern

Those signals are not mixed. You have to learn how to read them.

Weeks of texts, then disappearing — that is not a person who is intentionally interested in you. That is a person managing your attention while their effort is somewhere else. The pattern is the message.

Real interest looks like consistent effort. It doesn’t mean “texting every minute” — it means showing up steadily and moving toward an actual meeting. If someone’s effort fluctuates wildly, you’re a backup plan, not the plan.

3. On the ones who refuse to meet

Have a FaceTime right away. Endless texting tells you nothing. You can fall for a version of someone over text that doesn’t exist in person. A 10-minute video call tells you more than two weeks of messages.

If they won’t hop on a video, that’s your answer.

AI picks: Q39 Oversharing trap   Q49 Dating with kids and a busy life   Q34 Compatibility questions
From: Marina   To: david@example.com
Subject: Your 3 personalized answers, David — from the quiz

David,

You’re not too open — you just haven’t learned where the line sits. Open isn’t the same as on. Here are the three answers most relevant to where you are right now.

1. On the oversharing

What you’re doing is giving premium access for free.

You overshare because you’re strong enough to go deep. But not everyone has earned the right to that depth — and the people who haven’t earned it usually don’t know what to do with it. Some get overwhelmed. Some take advantage. Most can’t match it.

First dates are for discovery, not disclosure. Stay curious about them. Let them work for the deeper layers. The right person will earn their way in — and that earning is part of how the bond gets strong.

2. On dating with kids and a busy life

You can absolutely do this — if both of you are into it.

Dating with kids and a founder’s schedule isn’t a barrier to a real relationship. It’s a filter. The right person will see your full life and want in. The wrong person will see the same life and treat it as an obstacle. Both reactions tell you something — quickly.

The mistake is hiding the kids and the schedule until later. Don’t. Lead with the truth of your life. The person who fits will fit it as it actually is — not the simplified version you sent on the first date.

3. On what to do instead of dumping

Switch from talking to asking. Five to seven good questions, asked with the right energy, will tell you more than fifty checklist questions ever could. Try these:

— What have you learned from your past relationships?
— What’s something you used to want that you’ve outgrown?
— What does a great Sunday look like for you?

While they answer, watch them. The body language, the pauses, what they choose to share, what they avoid — those are the answers underneath the answers. You don’t need to fill the silence. Let them.

Maya,

You’re describing two patterns that look like one. The intensity isn’t the problem — the projection on top of it is. Here are the three answers most relevant to where you are right now.

1. On being “too much”

You are not too much. You feel things deeper than they’re comfortable feeling — that’s a different problem entirely.

That isn’t a flaw — that’s earned weight. It only feels like “too much” when you’re standing next to people who haven’t done the work to hold it. You will feel this way until you meet the right person — someone who isn’t threatened by your weight, but grateful for it.

Until then: don’t shrink. Filter.

2. On the “planning him too much”

What you’re really doing is falling for who he could be, before you’ve seen who he is.

After each date, check in with yourself: do I feel calm? Stable? Curious for more? If yes, go again. If no, listen to that. Stop falling for the future version. Date who they are right now. If the version in front of you isn’t enough, then they aren’t — and no amount of planning is going to change that.

3. On the difference between intensity and the right person

Listen to your body carefully. Butterflies in your stomach are not what you’re looking for — that flutter is anxiety dressed up as romance.

Real connection feels different. It feels like calm. Like safety. Like nothing is at stake because you already trust the ground.

If a pattern keeps producing butterflies, racing thoughts, and over-planning — that’s the pattern to break. If a pattern produces ease, presence, and steadiness — that’s the type to embrace.

Tom,

Honey, you’re not broken. You’re running an old program that nobody trained you out of — and the “should be married by now” story is making it worse. Here are the three answers most relevant to where you are right now.

1. On pulling away when it gets serious

You get cold feet because — underneath all of it — you don’t believe this can actually happen to you. You don’t yet feel worth it, and you haven’t built the knowledge for it.

Think about it this way: for your career, you have the experience, the reps, the moments where it was hard and you knew what to do. For a relationship? Nobody gave you a degree. Nobody trained you. So the minute it gets real, you default to the only skill you have: pulling away.

You don’t stop this with willpower. You stop it by learning — by building the knowledge and the inner belief that you’re someone who keeps the thing, not someone who escapes it.

2. On “I should be married by now”

You are not running out of time. None of us control how much time we get — yours is no shorter than anyone else’s, and the math doesn’t change just because the feeling is loud.

The “should be married by now” voice is what makes you settle — or, in your case, makes you panic at the idea that this could be it and pull the cord. The settling is one symptom. The pulling is another. The wound underneath is the same: a cracked self-image.

The fix is not to date faster. It’s to get clearer on two things: what you actually want, and how valuable you actually are. When both of those become solid, the urgency goes away.

3. On feeling broken

You’re not bad at this. You’re good at everything that doesn’t touch your emotions. The moment emotions come online, you start seeing your flaws under a magnifying glass. They look enormous to you. They don’t look that way to anyone else.

You don’t “crack” this area with a better strategy. You change your state of mind — what you feed it, what you listen to, what you say to yourself. When the internal picture changes, the external one follows.

From: Marina   To: rachel@example.com
Subject: Your 3 personalized answers, Rachel — from the quiz

Rachel,

Your friends might be wrong, and you might be wrong — both at the same time. The question isn’t whether your list is too long. It’s which items belong on it. Here are the three answers most relevant to where you are right now.

1. On the test for your list

Here’s the question that cuts through it. Do you yourself qualify for all those qualities you’re asking from the other person?

If yes — keep your standards. Expect no less than what you bring. If no — rethink them. You may be asking for more than you can give. The asymmetry is what’s keeping you single, not the list itself.

When you start dating someone real, see if you can let go of some of the requirements — and check if it sits okay with you. The ones you can release are the ones that were never deal-breakers in the first place. The ones you can’t are the real ones. If they fail those, move on.

2. On standards vs unrealistic

It’s simpler than people make it.

High standards are grounded — in reality and in self-awareness. They come from knowing what you bring, what you need, and what actually works for you.

Unrealistic standards are detached from reality. They’re built on ego or fear: ego that says “I deserve someone perfect,” or fear that says “if I keep raising the bar, no one can hurt me.” If your standards are protecting you from disappointment, they’re not standards — they’re armor.

3. On the settling test

When someone good shows up and you’re wondering if it’s settling, ask yourself this:

If this person never improved — same habits, same personality, same effort — would I still deeply want this life? Not “could I tolerate it.” Want it.

If the answer is no, you’re settling. If the answer is yes, you’re being realistic. That single question cuts through almost every doubt — and it tells you which items on your list were real, and which were noise.
